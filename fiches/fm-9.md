## 1. Private equity in one page

**Private equity** is equity invested in unlisted companies, held for years, sold for a gain. It is illiquid, it usually comes with control, and only the exit value counts.

- **LP (limited partners)** commit the capital: pension funds, endowments, insurers, family offices. Funds live 10-12 years.
- **GP (general partner)** picks and manages the deals, for **1-2% management fees + 20% carried interest** above a hurdle (the carry is earned only once LPs got their minimum return).
- Where LBOs sit in the life cycle: the **maturity phase**, when cash flows are stable enough to carry debt. Start-ups run on equity; turnarounds are a different fund.

<div class="key"><b>Remember:</b> an LBO fund buys mature, predictable businesses because their cash flow is what repays the acquisition debt.</div>

## 2. The three engines of value creation (A-L-O)

Every euro of sponsor return comes from one of three engines. On an exam, name the engine before you calculate.

| Engine | What it is | One-line example |
|---|---|---|
| **A - Arbitrage** | Buy low, sell high: the exit multiple beats the entry multiple | In at 7x, out at 9x with the same EBITDA |
| **L - Leverage** | The target's cash flow repays the debt, turning enterprise value into equity value | Debt falls 300 to 150 while EV is flat |
| **O - Operational** | Margins and revenue improve: the business itself is worth more | EBITDA grows 80 to 100 at a constant multiple |

<div class="key"><b>Remember:</b> only A and O create value. Leverage creates nothing by itself: it amplifies whatever A and O produce, gains and losses alike.</div>

## 3. What an LBO is, and what makes a good target

An **LBO** is an acquisition financed mostly with debt: the sponsor writes a thin equity cheque (typically 20-40%), lenders provide the rest (60-80%), and **the target's own cash flow repays the debt** over a ~5-year hold. The sponsor then sells at a higher EBITDA, a higher multiple, or simply with less debt.

A good target, in three groups:

- **Cheap to buy:** undervalued vs peers, under-leveraged, excess cash on the balance sheet.
- **Room to improve:** margins or growth lag peers, a catalyst is waiting, management can execute (or be upgraded).
- **Can carry debt:** stable and predictable cash flows, high barriers to entry, moderate capex and R&D, assets that could be sold if needed.

<div class="key"><b>Remember:</b> a biotech startup with negative, volatile cash flow is the worst LBO candidate: no cash flow, no debt capacity, no deal.</div>

## 4. Why leverage amplifies the equity return

Same company: bought 100, sold 180 five years later, debt held constant. Only the funding mix changes.

| | Equity in | Equity out | MOIC |
|---|---|---|---|
| All-equity | 100 | 180 | 1.8x |
| 50% debt | 50 | 130 | 2.6x |
| 70% debt | 30 | 110 | 3.7x |

Two mechanisms stack up:

- Each euro of debt repaid converts enterprise value into equity value, automatically.
- A smaller equity cheque means the same value creation lands on fewer euros of equity.

The mirror image: if the exit value falls, leverage multiplies the loss just as fast.

## 5. The players and the four exits

- **Sponsor** (the PE fund): equity and control. **Lenders**: banks and bond investors, the debt tranches. **NewCo/BidCo**: the vehicle that borrows, buys, then merges with the target. **Management**: often rolls over equity and runs the plan.

| Exit | What happens | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Strategic sale | Sold to a corporate buyer | Often the best price: synergies |
| Secondary buyout | Sold to another sponsor | The next fund sees more value to create |
| IPO | Listed, shares sold down over time | Needs a receptive market |
| Dividend recap | The company re-levers and pays a dividend | Cash back without a full exit |

## 6. The LBO model in eight steps

One disciplined sequence. Each step feeds the next.

**Step 1 - Price the deal.** Private target: Entry EV = entry multiple x normalised EBITDA. Our deal: EBITDA 409.0 x 8.5 = EV 3,476.5 (no existing net debt).

**Step 2 - Sources & uses.** Uses: purchase price 3,476.5 + fees 52.1 (1.5%) = 3,528.6. Sources: TLB 1,308.8 + senior notes 613.5 + subordinated notes 409.0 = debt 2,331.3 (66%) + **sponsor equity 1,197.3 (34%)**. Equity is always the plug: it fills whatever debt does not fund, and the two columns must balance.

**Step 3 - Set the debt stack.** Cheapest and most senior at the top, sized in turns of EBITDA:

| Layer | Pricing | Profile |
|---|---|---|
| Revolver (RCF) | SOFR + 200 | Senior, undrawn at close, working-capital backstop |
| Term Loan A | SOFR + 300 | Senior secured, amortises ~20%/yr, ~5 yrs |
| Term Loan B | SOFR + 350 | Senior secured, ~1%/yr + bullet, ~7 yrs |
| Senior notes | ~10% fixed | Unsecured, bullet, callable at a premium |
| Subordinated notes | ~12% fixed | Longest, first loss after equity |
| Sponsor equity | the residual | Junior, first loss, all the upside |

Pricing steps up about 100 bps per junior layer, plus ~50 bps for longer maturity. Total leverage here: 5.7x EBITDA.

**Steps 4-5 - Project and derive the cash.** Project the P&L (growth, margins), then:

<div class="formula">Levered FCF = EBITDA - maintenance capex - change in NWC - cash interest - cash taxes</div>

**Step 6 - The debt schedule and the cash sweep.** Cash goes out in strict order:

1. Mandatory amortisation first: TLA ~20%/yr, TLB ~1%/yr.
2. The sweep prepays the TLA until it is gone.
3. Then the sweep prepays the TLB.
4. Bonds stay outstanding (bullets, callable at a premium); leftover cash builds up.

Our deal: debt falls 2,331.3 to 1,668.8 in five years (5.7x to 3.7x EBITDA) - only the TLB moves, from 1,308.8 to 646.3.

**Step 7 - Circularity.** Interest depends on debt, debt depends on the sweep, the sweep depends on interest. Enable iterative calculation and let the loop settle.

**Step 8 - Returns.** Next two sections.

## 7. Multiples: choose them and apply them

<div class="formula">Multiple = price of the asset / profit generated by the asset</div>

| Multiple | Use it when | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| EV/EBITDA | The LBO default; cyclical sectors | Ignores capex and tax differences |
| EV/EBIT | Capital-heavy, comparable depreciation | Accounting policies |
| P/E | Profitable companies, forward EPS available | Distorted by leverage |
| EV/Sales | Negative earnings, early stage | Partial picture only |
| Price/Book | Financials, capital-intensive | Accounting-driven |

Three working rules:

- **Consistency first.** EV pairs with asset-side profits (EBITDA, EBIT); equity price pairs with equity-side profits (net income, EPS). Mixing the two is the classic error.
- **Define before you compare.** Trailing vs forward, basic vs diluted, before vs after extraordinary items.
- **Peers on fundamentals, not labels.** Same growth and margins, or not a peer.

<div class="example"><b>Example (Bulgari-LVMH):</b> screening 15 luxury names on category, growth and margins keeps only the true peers. Relevant comps value Bulgari at 7.3-13.7 per share against 5.6-9.7 on the full sample. The peer set moves the price more than the method.</div>

## 8. Returns: IRR, MOIC and the five levers

- **IRR** is annualised: it sees timing. **MOIC** (cash-on-cash) is cash in divided by cash out: it ignores timing.
- Report both. The same 1.8x reached one year earlier means a higher IRR.

Base case of our deal: **12.1% IRR / 1.8x MOIC** (8.5x in, 8.5x out, five years). Exit a year early: 9.6%. Exit one turn lower (7.5x): 6.9%.

The five levers, mapped to the engines:

| Lever | Engine | Direction |
|---|---|---|
| Purchase price | A | Pay less, earn more: the strongest lever |
| Exit multiple | A | Sell higher |
| Leverage | L | Smaller cheque, same outcome split harder |
| Revenue growth | O | Bigger EBITDA at exit |
| EBITDA margin | O | More cash per euro of sales |

## 9. A full deal, entry to exit (class workbook)

One clean pass through the model, numbers rounded. Entry EBITDA 100, entry multiple 10x, existing net debt 30, fees 2% of EV.

- **Step 1 - Price it.** EV = 100 x 10 = 1,000. Seller proceeds = 1,000 - 30 = 970.
- **Step 2 - Fund it.** Uses = 970 + 30 (refinance) + 20 (fees) = 1,020. New debt = 4 x 100 = 400. Sponsor equity = 1,020 - 400 = **620** (the plug).
- **Step 3 - Project.** Revenue 250 growing 10%/yr at a 40% EBITDA margin. Exit EBITDA = 250 x 1.1^5 x 40% = **161.1**.
- **Step 4 - Repay.** Taxes 40%, capex 4.5% of revenue, NWC 15% of the revenue increase, interest 10% on beginning debt. Debt falls 400 to **173.3** by exit.
- **Step 5 - Exit at 11x.** EV = 161.1 x 11 = 1,771.6. Sponsor proceeds = 1,771.6 - 173.3 = **1,598.2**.
- **Step 6 - Measure.** MOIC = 1,598.2 / 620 = **2.58x**. IRR = 2.58^(1/5) - 1 = **20.9%**.

Where does the 978.2 of gain come from? The attribution must reconcile (gains shown to the cent):

| Driver | Calculation | Gain |
|---|---|---|
| EBITDA growth | (161.05 - 100) x 10 | 610.51 |
| Multiple expansion | (11 - 10) x 161.05 | 161.05 |
| Debt repaid | 400 - 173.32 | 226.68 |
| Fees | entry fee | -20.00 |
| **Total** | | **978.24** |

Sensitivity is not a promise: at 4x leverage, IRR runs 15.5% to 23.2% for exit multiples 9x to 12x; at the 11x exit, 3x leverage gives 19.2% and 7x gives 30.2%, with proportionally more downside risk.

## 10. Is the structure safe?

Three ratio families, tested every year. A healthy LBO gets safer as it ages.

- **Leverage:** Net debt/EBITDA (safe: at or below ~6x) and Senior secured/EBITDA (safe: at or below ~4x).
- **Coverage:** EBITDA/interest and (EBITDA - capex)/interest.
- **Liquidity:** cash on hand + undrawn revolver.

Our deal: net debt/EBITDA falls 5.6x to 3.7x over the hold - inside the safe zone and improving.

## 11. Exam traps

- Saying leverage creates value. It amplifies; only A and O create.
- Forgetting equity is the plug in sources & uses.
- Sweeping the bonds: the sweep prepays term loans only; notes are bullets, callable at a premium.
- Mixing numerator and denominator (EV/net income, equity value/EBITDA).
- Comparing multiples with different definitions (trailing vs forward, basic vs diluted).
- Building peers on sector labels instead of growth and margins.
- Targeting volatile, capex-heavy, early-stage companies for an LBO.
- Reading MOIC as if it captured timing; that is what IRR is for.
