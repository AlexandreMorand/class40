## 1. Identify the risk and the currency exposure

- A firm faces several connected risks: market, credit, liquidity, operational, foreign exchange, interest rate, business, reputational and commodity risk.
- Risk management starts by identifying what can change, which cash flow it affects, and when the firm must pay or collect it.
- The A2A example distinguishes liquidity risk, derating risk, interest rate risk and sustainability covenant risk. Its responses include financial planning and pre-funding, asset rotation and equity/hybrid financing, fixed/variable-rate debt management, and a sustainable financial committee.

<div class="def"><b>Transaction exposure:</b> gains or losses from settling existing financial obligations whose terms are stated in a foreign currency. It concerns contractual cash flows, not simply a forecast of future sales.</div>

- Examples: a sale invoiced in GBP by a EUR-based firm; a USD-based firm's GBP supplier payable; foreign-currency loan repayment; assets or liabilities denominated in a foreign currency.
- **Operating/economic exposure:** expected future cash flows may change because exchange-rate movements affect international competitiveness.
- **Translation/accounting exposure:** FX volatility affects consolidation of subsidiaries with different currencies.
- Do not treat these as interchangeable. This lecture's contractual derivatives primarily manage **transaction exposure**.

<div class="def"><b>Receivable:</b> foreign currency the firm will receive. The firm is hurt if that currency loses value in home currency.<br/><b>Payable:</b> foreign currency the firm must pay. The firm is hurt if that currency becomes more expensive in home currency.</div>

- Hedging reduces the variability of expected cash flows. It is not a promise that the hedged outcome will beat the unhedged outcome in every scenario.
- A treasury decision depends on risk tolerance, the firm's exchange-rate view and its confidence in that view. Removing downside also has a cost: a premium or forgone favourable movements.

## 2. Read exchange rates and forward prices

- **Spot market:** almost immediate purchase or sale of foreign exchange.
- **Forward market:** agree today to buy or sell foreign exchange on a future date at a fixed price. In the lecture's setup, no cash changes hands at entry; exchange occurs at maturity.
- Write the units before calculating. In S<sub>j/k</sub>, the quotation is currency j per one unit of currency k.
- For a European investor, **direct quotation** is EUR/USD: euros per dollar. **Indirect quotation** is USD/EUR: dollars per euro.

<div class="formula">Direct rate = 1 / Indirect rate<br/>EUR proceeds = USD amount / (USD/EUR rate) = USD amount × (EUR/USD rate)</div>

Where:

- The USD amount is the foreign-currency cash flow.
- The indirect rate is dollars per euro; its reciprocal is euros per dollar.

What this does:

- First check the denominator of the quote. If one euro buys 1.60 dollars, one dollar buys 1/1.60 = EUR 0.625.
- Then convert the cash flow: USD 100 / 1.60 = EUR 62.50.
- If USD/EUR rises, the euro strengthens and a fixed USD receivable yields **fewer euros**. If EUR/USD rises, the dollar strengthens and it yields **more euros**.

### 2.1 Forward premium or discount

<div class="formula">f<sub>N,j</sub> = [(F<sub>N</sub> - S) / S] × (360 / N)</div>

Where:

- S is today's spot quote, in USD per unit of currency j (American terms).
- F<sub>N</sub> is the N-day forward quote, using exactly the same units.
- N is the maturity in days; the lecture uses a 360-day year.
- f is an annualised decimal. Multiply by 100 to express it as a percentage.

What this does:

- Subtract spot from forward to find the price difference.
- Divide by spot to make it a relative difference.
- Multiply by 360/N to annualise. Positive means a premium for j in this quotation; negative means a discount.

<div class="example"><b>EUR example from Exhibit 5.7:</b> S = USD 1.1235/EUR and six-month F = USD 1.1299/EUR. Using N = 180 days: [(1.1299 - 1.1235)/1.1235] × 2 = 0.011393, or <b>1.1393% annualised premium</b>.</div>

- Do not reverse the quote halfway through. Reversing a quotation changes which currency's premium/discount you are describing.
- Keep enough decimals before rounding: the yen quotes in the displayed table are rounded, so they do not reproduce the slide's 0.460% answer exactly.

## 3. Match the hedge to a receivable or a payable

Use a **direct quote** throughout this section: S<sub>T</sub>, F and K are home currency per unit of foreign currency. Q is the foreign-currency amount; p<sub>T</sub> is the premium per foreign unit, expressed in home currency at the comparison date T.

| Cash flow | No hedge | Forward | Bought option |
|---|---|---|---|
| Receive foreign currency | Sell Q at spot at T | Short forward: sell Q at F | Put: right to sell Q at K |
| Pay foreign currency | Buy Q at spot at T | Long forward: buy Q at F | Call: right to buy Q at K |

<div class="formula">Receivable: unhedged proceeds = Q × S<sub>T</sub><br/>Forward proceeds = Q × F<br/>Put-hedged net proceeds = Q × [max(S<sub>T</sub>, K) - p<sub>T</sub>]</div>

What this does:

- The exporter sells the foreign currency. Choose the **higher selling price**, spot or strike.
- Exercise the put when S<sub>T</sub> &lt; K; otherwise sell at spot. At equality, either gives the same gross amount.
- Deduct the premium in both branches. The minimum net proceeds are Q × (K - p<sub>T</sub>).

<div class="formula">Payable: unhedged cost = Q × S<sub>T</sub><br/>Forward cost = Q × F<br/>Call-hedged total cost = Q × [min(S<sub>T</sub>, K) + p<sub>T</sub>]</div>

What this does:

- The importer buys the foreign currency. Choose the **lower buying price**, spot or strike.
- Exercise the call when S<sub>T</sub> &gt; K; otherwise buy at spot.
- Add the premium in both branches. The maximum total cost is Q × (K + p<sub>T</sub>).

### 3.1 Compare cash flows at the same date

- A premium is paid today, but the receivable/payable settles later. If the example accounts for financing/opportunity cost, convert the premium to its future value before comparing maturity cash flows.
- With a simple annual rate r and horizon t years, p<sub>T</sub> = p<sub>0</sub> × (1 + r × t). Here p<sub>0</sub> is today's premium per unit in home currency.
- First multiply the premium by the stated time factor; then subtract it from proceeds or add it to costs. Do not change the strike when doing this.
- **Exercise threshold** depends on strike K, not on premium. **Forward-option break-even** compares net cash flows and therefore includes the premium.

### 3.2 Understand the forward payoff, not just the contract

- A long forward has payoff Q × (S<sub>T</sub> - F); a short forward has payoff Q × (F - S<sub>T</sub>). These are gains/losses relative to the maturity spot price, in home currency.
- First find the spot-forward difference, then multiply by Q. A forward's standalone loss can be offset by a favourable change in the underlying commercial cash flow.
- In the GBP 100m importer example, F = USD 1.50/GBP. At S<sub>T</sub> = 1.80, the unhedged purchase costs USD 180m and the long forward gains USD 30m. Net cost = USD 150m.
- At S<sub>T</sub> = 1.20, the purchase costs USD 120m but the forward loses USD 30m. Net cost is still USD 150m. The hedge removes FX variation, not the purchase itself.

## 4. Aidan: a GBP receivable in EUR

- Aidan is a European exporter receiving **GBP 1,000,000 in 90 days** for equipment sold to a British customer.
- Use the slide and workbook inputs: spot EUR 1.09/GBP; forward EUR 1.076/GBP; put strike EUR 1.07/GBP; premium 1.5%; annual cost of capital 12%.
- A weaker pound reduces euro proceeds. The correct forward is **short GBP**; the correct option is a **bought GBP put**.

### 4.1 Forward hedge

- Sell GBP 1,000,000 forward at EUR 1.076/GBP.
- Maturity proceeds = 1,000,000 × 1.076 = **EUR 1,076,000**, whatever the maturity spot rate.
- This fixes the euro receipt, but gives up the benefit of a stronger pound.

### 4.2 Put option hedge

- Premium today = 1,000,000 × 1.5% × 1.09 = **EUR 16,350**.
- The percentage premium is applied to the notional converted at today's spot, not directly subtracted as EUR 0.015/GBP.
- Three-month future-value premium = 16,350 × (1 + 12%/4) = **EUR 16,840.50**.
- Per-pound maturity premium = 16,840.50 / 1,000,000 = **EUR 0.0168405/GBP**.

<div class="formula">Net proceeds = 1,000,000 × max(S<sub>T</sub>, 1.07) - 16,840.50<br/>Floor = EUR 1,053,159.50<br/>Forward-option break-even S* = 1.076 + 0.0168405 = EUR 1.0928405/GBP</div>

What this does:

- First choose spot or strike, whichever yields more euros.
- Multiply by the GBP receivable and deduct the premium at maturity.
- To find break-even above the strike, set 1,000,000 × S* - 16,840.50 equal to EUR 1,076,000. Solve for S*.

| S<sub>T</sub> (EUR/GBP) | Unhedged (EUR) | Forward (EUR) | Put net (EUR) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.00 | 1,000,000 | 1,076,000 | 1,053,159.50 |
| 1.07 | 1,070,000 | 1,076,000 | 1,053,159.50 |
| 1.10 | 1,100,000 | 1,076,000 | 1,083,159.50 |
| 1.20 | 1,200,000 | 1,076,000 | 1,183,159.50 |

- The put is exercised below **1.07**, not below the forecast rate 1.085. The forecast is a view, not a contractual exercise threshold.
- Below 1.0928405, the forward gives higher proceeds; above it, the put gives higher proceeds. This comparison assumes the stated premium convention and same notional/date.

### 4.3 The money-market line in the source chart

- The source chart also shows a money-market alternative. Borrow GBP now against the future receivable, convert to EUR today, then carry the euros to maturity.
- Using the slide's GBP borrowing rate of 10% annually: GBP borrowed = 1,000,000 / 1.025 = GBP 975,609.76. Conversion at 1.09 gives EUR 1,063,414.63 today.
- The chart's EUR 1,095,317 uses a **3% three-month opportunity-return assumption**: 1,063,414.63 × 1.03 = EUR 1,095,317.07.
- Investing instead at the stated EUR deposit rate of 6% annually gives 1,063,414.63 × 1.015 = EUR 1,079,365.85. The different totals reflect different euro carrying assumptions, not a different FX hedge.

## 5. Boeing: compare forwards and options in USD

### 5.1 Exporter: GBP 10m receivable in one year

- Boeing sells to British Airways and will receive **GBP 10,000,000**.
- Inputs: F = K = USD 1.46/GBP; put premium = USD 0.02/GBP.
- The slide's comparison deducts the stated premium directly; no financing adjustment is supplied for this example.

<div class="formula">Forward receipt = 10,000,000 × 1.46 = USD 14,600,000<br/>Put net receipt = 10,000,000 × [max(S<sub>T</sub>, 1.46) - 0.02]<br/>Put floor = USD 14,400,000<br/>Break-even S* = 1.46 + 0.02 = USD 1.48/GBP</div>

What this does:

- Below 1.46, exercise the put and receive USD 14.6m gross, then deduct USD 0.2m premium.
- Above 1.46, sell at spot and still deduct the premium.
- At spot 1.48, put net proceeds equal the forward's USD 14.6m. Above 1.48, the put yields more; below it, the forward yields more.

| S<sub>T</sub> (USD/GBP) | Forward receipt | Put net receipt |
|---|---|---|
| 1.40 | USD 14.60m | USD 14.40m |
| 1.48 | USD 14.60m | USD 14.60m |
| 1.55 | USD 14.60m | USD 15.30m |

### 5.2 Importer: GBP 5m payable in one year

- This is a **different transaction**: Boeing imports an engine and owes **GBP 5,000,000**.
- Inputs: spot USD 1.80/GBP; forward USD 1.75/GBP; call strike USD 1.80/GBP; premium USD 0.018/GBP; US annual rate 6%.
- Buy GBP forward (**long**) or buy a GBP **call**. A put would protect the wrong direction.
- Upfront premium = 5,000,000 × 0.018 = **USD 90,000**.
- Future-value premium = 90,000 × 1.06 = **USD 95,400**, or USD 0.01908/GBP.

<div class="formula">Forward cost = 5,000,000 × 1.75 = USD 8,750,000<br/>Call total cost = 5,000,000 × min(S<sub>T</sub>, 1.80) + 95,400<br/>Maximum call cost = USD 9,095,400<br/>Break-even S* = 1.75 - 0.01908 = USD 1.73092/GBP</div>

What this does:

- First choose the cheaper way to buy GBP: spot or strike.
- Multiply by GBP 5m and add the maturity premium.
- Below the strike, set 5,000,000 × S* + 95,400 equal to USD 8,750,000, then solve for S*.

| S<sub>T</sub> (USD/GBP) | Forward cost | Call total cost |
|---|---|---|
| 1.65 | USD 8,750,000 | USD 8,345,400 |
| 1.73092 | USD 8,750,000 | USD 8,750,000 |
| 1.80 or above | USD 8,750,000 | USD 9,095,400 |

- The call is cheaper than the forward below 1.73092; the forward is cheaper above it. The call is exercised only above 1.80.
- Premium paid today (90,000), premium at maturity (95,400), break-even (1.73092) and strike (1.80) are four different quantities.

## 6. Over-hedging: separate currency risk from quantity risk

<div class="def"><b>Over-hedging:</b> the hedge notional exceeds the actual exposure. The excess derivative is no longer offset by the commercial cash flow and becomes a speculative position.</div>

- At **T0, budget**, a European exporter expects USD 100 revenue, costs EUR 70 and an indirect budget rate of USD 1.30/EUR.
- The slide rounds the reciprocal to EUR 0.77/USD, giving a budget margin of EUR 7. Using the exact reciprocal gives 100/1.30 - 70 = **EUR 6.9231**.
- With revenue fixed at USD 100, a weaker dollar at USD 1.60/EUR gives a margin of 100/1.60 - 70 = **EUR -7.50**. A stronger dollar at USD 1.00/EUR gives **EUR 30**.
- These are currency effects **assuming the revenue amount remains USD 100**.

### 6.1 What happens when the amount also changes

- The CFO sells **USD 100 forward** at USD 1.30/EUR at T0.
- At **T1, invoicing**, actual sales are only **USD 50**. The remaining USD 50 forward commitment is now uncovered.
- At **T2, collection**, spot is USD 1.00/EUR. The firm collects USD 50, but still must deliver USD 100 under the forward.

| Step at T2 | EUR cash flow |
|---|---|
| Convert collected USD 50 at forward: 50 / 1.30 | +38.4615 |
| Buy missing USD 50 at spot: 50 / 1.00 | -50.0000 |
| Deliver these USD 50 at forward: 50 / 1.30 | +38.4615 |
| Net FX cash receipts | +26.9231 |
| Operating costs | -70.0000 |
| Final margin | **-43.0769** |

- The excess hedge alone loses **EUR 11.5385**: EUR 38.4615 received minus EUR 50 paid to acquire the missing dollars.
- Actual sales converted at the forward yield EUR 38.4615. After the excess-hedge loss and costs, the final margin is EUR -43.0769, approximately the slide's EUR -43.
- The decline from exact budget margin EUR 6.9231 to EUR -43.0769 is **EUR 50**. It is **not all a derivative loss**.
- Decomposition: revenue shortfall at the locked rate = 50/1.30 = EUR 38.4615; extra hedge loss = EUR 11.5385; total deterioration = EUR 50.
- Against leaving the actual USD 50 unhedged at spot 1.00, the final margin would have been EUR -20. The hedged margin is worse by EUR 23.0769: forgone appreciation on collected dollars plus the uncovered-forward loss.

### 6.2 Design the hedge around the exposure

- Ask **when** to hedge: at budget, the cash flow is still uncertain; at invoicing, the contractual amount is clearer.
- Ask **how much** to hedge: hedging 100% of a forecast removes price uncertainty on that notional but does not remove quantity uncertainty.
- Keep reviewing the commercial cash flow and hedge amount together. A derivative can become speculative when the underlying sale disappears or shrinks.

## 7. Recap: budget, invoicing and collection

- A European exporter expects and invoices **USD 100**. Collection occurs at T2.
- Two T2 scenarios: **USD 1.40/EUR** (stronger dollar, favourable to the exporter) and **USD 1.75/EUR** (weaker dollar, unfavourable).
- Hedge choices: T0 forward at USD 1.54/EUR; T1 forward at USD 1.65/EUR; or a T0 put protecting sale of USD at the strike.
- Use the **worked solution's precise T0/strike quote, USD 1.5758/EUR**, consistently. Its reciprocal is **EUR 0.6345983/USD**. The earlier setup's 1.57 is a different, less precise input and must not be mixed with the more precise solution.

### 7.1 Premium and net option proceeds

- Premium is USD 0.02 **per dollar sold**, so total premium is USD 2.
- Convert that premium at T0: USD 2 / 1.5758 = **EUR 1.2692**. This exercise deducts that cost directly, without a financing adjustment.
- Direct strike k = 1/1.5758 = EUR 0.6345983/USD. Direct maturity spot s<sub>T</sub> = 1/R<sub>T</sub>, where R<sub>T</sub> is USD/EUR.

<div class="formula">Put net proceeds = 100 × max(s<sub>T</sub>, k) - 1.2691966<br/>Option floor = 100/1.5758 - 2/1.5758 = EUR 62.1906</div>

What this does:

- Convert the indirect quote to euros per dollar before comparing spot with strike.
- At R<sub>T</sub> = 1.75, each dollar buys fewer euros than the strike: exercise the put.
- At R<sub>T</sub> = 1.40, spot gives more euros: let the put expire and sell at spot.
- Deduct the same EUR 1.2692 premium in both cases.

| Strategy: realised EUR revenue | R<sub>T</sub> = 1.40 | R<sub>T</sub> = 1.75 |
|---|---|---|
| No hedge: 100/R<sub>T</sub> | 71.4286 | 57.1429 |
| T0 forward: 100/1.54 | 64.9351 | 64.9351 |
| T1 forward: 100/1.65 | 60.6061 | 60.6061 |
| Put: best sale rate minus premium | 70.1594 | 62.1906 |

- Net effective EUR/USD rates for the option: **0.701594** in the favourable scenario and **0.621906** at the floor.
- Their USD/EUR reciprocals are **1.425326** and **1.607959**. An effective rate is net proceeds divided by notional, not the market spot quote.
- Consistent budget gross revenue at 1.5758 is **EUR 63.4598**. The standalone EUR 62.5 budget label on the final slide is not the conversion of USD 100 at 1.5758.
- The forward produces a fixed receipt. The put has a lower guaranteed net floor than the T0 forward but preserves favourable FX movements after paying its premium.
- The T1 forward locks in less revenue because 1.65 dollars are now needed to buy one euro. It still protects against further adverse moves; a lower locked amount does not by itself make a hedge pointless.

## 8. Common mistakes & exam traps

- **Multiplying by an indirect quote:** USD to EUR uses USD ÷ (USD/EUR). Multiplication is correct only with EUR/USD.
- **Calling a higher USD/EUR quote a stronger dollar:** it is a stronger euro. The dollar receivable yields fewer euros.
- **Wrong hedge direction:** receivable = short forward or bought put; payable = long forward or bought call on the foreign currency.
- **Confusing strike with break-even:** exercise depends on strike; whether the option beats a forward depends on premium and forward rate too.
- **Subtracting premium from importer cost:** a bought option adds cost. For an exporter it reduces net proceeds.
- **Mixing today's and maturity money:** apply the stated future-value premium in Aidan and Boeing's importer case; use the direct premium convention of the Boeing exporter and recap examples.
- **Changing the example's currencies:** Aidan's outcomes are EUR; Boeing's are USD. A copied "euro/GBP" label does not change Boeing's USD cash flows.
- **Treating all budget deterioration as over-hedging loss:** separate missing revenue, lost favourable FX opportunity on actual revenue, and the uncovered derivative's loss.
- **Rounding inputs too early:** keep full precision in reciprocals and premium calculations; round final cash amounts. Approximate slide labels are not new inputs.
- **Reading a derivative payoff as the total business cash flow:** the hedge gain/loss offsets the underlying exposure; the invoice remains payable or receivable.
