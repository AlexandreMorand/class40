## 1. Why a foreign sale creates currency risk

### 1.1 Start with the business transaction

- A European firm sells equipment for **GBP 1 million**, payable in three months. Its customer owes a fixed number of pounds, but the firm measures its cash and expenses in euros.
- The contractual pound amount is fixed. The number of euros those pounds will buy is not fixed until conversion, unless the firm hedges.
- If the pound weakens before collection, the same sale yields fewer euros. The customer can pay in full and the seller can still receive less home-currency cash than expected.
- The problem is to manage the **currency value of a payment**, not to change the commercial sale or eliminate every business risk.

<div class="def"><b>Transaction exposure:</b> gains or losses arising from settlement of existing financial obligations whose terms are stated in a foreign currency. This is a cash-flow exposure attached to an existing contract.</div>

### 1.2 Separate the three FX exposures

| Exposure | What can change? | Distinguishing feature |
|---|---|---|
| Transaction | Home-currency value of outstanding foreign-currency obligations | Existing contractual receipts or payments |
| Operating/economic | Future cash flows as FX changes international competitiveness | Future business performance, not just one invoice |
| Translation/accounting | Reported figures when foreign subsidiaries are consolidated | Currency conversion in consolidation |

- Foreign-currency purchases, sales, borrowing, lending, assets and liabilities can create transaction exposure.
- A derivative matching an invoice can protect that transaction. It does not automatically protect future competitiveness or all consolidated accounts.

## 2. Read the exchange rate before calculating

### 2.1 A quotation is a price with units

- The **spot market** involves almost immediate purchase or sale of foreign exchange.
- S<sub>j/k</sub> means the price of **one unit of currency k in currency j**. The denominator is what is priced; the numerator is what pays for it.
- EUR 1.09/GBP means one pound costs 1.09 euros. USD 1.60/EUR means one euro costs 1.60 dollars. These price different currencies and cannot be applied in the same way.

<div class="def"><b>Direct quotation, for a European investor:</b> EUR per foreign unit, such as EUR/USD.<br/><b>Indirect quotation:</b> foreign units per EUR, such as USD/EUR.<br/>The investor's perspective matters: USD/EUR is indirect for the European investor but direct for a US investor.</div>

<div class="formula">Direct quotation = 1 / Indirect quotation<br/>EUR per USD = 1 / (USD per EUR)</div>

Where both quotations describe the same currencies at the same time. Taking a reciprocal reverses the units, not the underlying market.

**Worked conversion: USD 100 at USD 1.60/EUR**

- **Step 1 - Read:** EUR 1 buys USD 1.60.
- **Step 2 - Reverse:** USD 1 buys EUR 1/1.60 = EUR 0.625.
- **Step 3 - Convert:** USD 100 × EUR 0.625/USD = **EUR 62.50**. Equivalently, USD 100 ÷ (USD 1.60/EUR) = EUR 62.50.
- **Step 4 - Check:** dollars cancel, leaving euros. Multiplication by USD/EUR instead would produce dollars squared per euro, not the wanted currency.

### 2.2 Tell which currency strengthened

| Indirect quotation | EUR value of USD 100 | Effect on a EUR-based exporter |
|---|---|---|
| USD 1.00/EUR | 100/1.00 = EUR 100 | Strong dollar: favourable |
| USD 1.30/EUR | 100/1.30 = EUR 76.9231 | Intermediate value |
| USD 1.60/EUR | 100/1.60 = EUR 62.50 | Weak dollar: unfavourable |

- When USD/EUR rises, a euro buys more dollars: **euro strengthens, dollar weakens**. A fixed dollar receipt buys fewer euros.
- When the reciprocal EUR/USD rises, each dollar buys more euros: **dollar strengthens**.
- Do not learn "a higher rate is good". Ask **higher in which quotation, and for a receipt or a payment?**

### 2.3 Identify the adverse movement

- **Receivable:** foreign currency to collect. The firm will **sell** it. Risk: its home-currency price falls.
- **Payable:** foreign currency to deliver. The firm must **buy** it. Risk: its home-currency price rises.
- General formulas below use a **direct quote: home currency per foreign unit**. Examples explicitly identify when an indirect quote is supplied.

## 3. Forwards: lock a price and understand the offset

### 3.1 What the contract obliges the firm to do

- A **forward** contracts today for a future purchase or sale at exchange rate F. In the lecture's setup, no money changes hands at entry; currencies are exchanged at maturity.
- **Long forward:** obligation to buy foreign currency. Matches a payable: the firm needs it for its supplier.
- **Short forward:** obligation to sell foreign currency. Matches a receivable: the firm collects it from its customer.
- A forward is binding even if the later spot price would be better.

<div class="formula">Short-forward-hedged receivable: receipt = Q × F<br/>Long-forward-hedged payable: cost = Q × F</div>

Where Q is foreign-currency amount and F is home currency per foreign unit. Amount and maturity must match the commercial cash flow for a full offset.

- First take the number of foreign units; multiply by the agreed price per unit.
- The result is the fixed home-currency amount. For a matched hedge, maturity spot no longer changes this receipt or payment.

### 3.2 Why a derivative loss can mean the hedge worked

The slide's US importer owes **GBP 100m in one year**. F = **USD 1.50/GBP**.

<div class="formula">Long-forward gain/loss relative to spot = Q × (S<sub>T</sub> - F)<br/>Short-forward gain/loss relative to spot = Q × (F - S<sub>T</sub>)</div>

Where S<sub>T</sub> is maturity spot, in the same units as F. Positive is a derivative gain; negative is a loss. Subtract the prices first, then multiply by the foreign notional.

| Calculation at maturity | Spot USD 1.80/GBP | Spot USD 1.20/GBP |
|---|---|---|
| Buy GBP 100m at spot | Cost USD 180m | Cost USD 120m |
| Long-forward gain/loss | 100m × (1.80 - 1.50) = +USD 30m | 100m × (1.20 - 1.50) = -USD 30m |
| Spot cost minus derivative gain | 180m - 30m = **USD 150m** | 120m - (-30m) = **USD 150m** |

- Stronger GBP makes the commercial purchase USD 30m more expensive; the derivative gains USD 30m and offsets it.
- Weaker GBP makes the purchase USD 30m cheaper; the derivative loses USD 30m and offsets that benefit.
- This decomposition explains the locked rate. With physical delivery, pay USD 150m for GBP 100m under the forward. **Do not count that physical payment and then add the payoff again.**
- A flat hedged-payoff line means no FX gain/loss relative to the locked benchmark. It does not mean the supplier costs zero.

### 3.3 Hedging versus speculation

- A long forward gains above F; a short gains below F. Both cross zero at F.
- The slide's USD/Swiss-franc diagram uses F = 1.0815. At spot 1.10, the long earns **0.0185 USD per franc**; at 1.05 it loses **0.0315**. The short has the opposite result.
- Without an offsetting commercial cash flow, this is **speculation**. With a matched exposure, judge the derivative and the business payment together.

### 3.4 Forward premium or discount

A forward price can differ from today's spot price. The lecture expresses that difference as an annualised percentage. It is a **quoted-price comparison**, not an option premium or realised hedge profit.

<div class="formula">f<sub>N,j</sub> = [(F<sub>N</sub> - S) / S] × (360 / N)</div>

Where:

- S is spot in **USD per unit of currency j**, American terms.
- F<sub>N</sub> is the N-day forward quote in the **same units**.
- N is days to maturity; the convention here is a 360-day year.
- f is an annualised decimal; multiply by 100 for a percentage.

**Worked example: EUR in the slide's exchange-rate table**

- **Step 1 - Match inputs:** S = USD 1.1235/EUR; six-month F = USD 1.1299/EUR; N = 180 days.
- **Step 2 - Difference:** 1.1299 - 1.1235 = **USD 0.0064/EUR**. EUR is priced higher forward than spot.
- **Step 3 - Relative difference:** 0.0064/1.1235 = **0.00569648**, or 0.569648% over six months.
- **Step 4 - Annualise:** 0.00569648 × (360/180) = **0.01139297**.
- **Step 5 - Interpret:** ×100 gives a **1.1393% annualised forward premium on EUR** in this USD/EUR quotation.

- F &gt; S means currency j has a premium in this quote; F &lt; S means a discount.
- Reversing the quote changes the currency whose premium you describe. Do not invert just one rate or label this result a premium on USD.
- Annualisation scales a period difference; it does not turn a six-month contract into a one-year contract.
- The lecture's three-month yen answer is **0.460% annualised**. Printed yen quotes are rounded and do not reproduce it exactly; preserve precision when an exercise provides it.

### 3.5 Certainty has a trade-off

- A forward removes conversion-price uncertainty on its matched amount. It does not guarantee a better outcome than staying unhedged.
- The firm gives up favourable FX movements in exchange for protection against adverse ones.
- Locking the price on GBP 1m does not ensure the company will actually sell enough goods to collect GBP 1m.
- An option replaces the obligation with a **choice**. That changes the protection from a fixed outcome to a floor or ceiling.

## 4. Options: insure the downside and retain upside

### 4.1 Right, strike and premium

- A **call** gives its buyer the right to buy foreign currency at strike K. An importer buys a call to protect against a higher purchase price.
- A **put** gives its buyer the right to sell at K. An exporter buys a put to protect against a lower selling price.
- The buyer can let the option expire when spot offers a better price. The **premium** buys this flexibility and is paid whether the option is exercised or not.
- These examples concern bought options: the firm purchases protection, rather than selling an option to someone else.

### 4.2 Exporter: a put sets a receipt floor

- The exporter collects Q foreign units. If spot S<sub>T</sub> &lt; K, exercise and sell at K. If S<sub>T</sub> &gt; K, sell at spot instead. At equality the gross receipt is the same.

<div class="formula">Put-hedged net receipt = Q × max(S<sub>T</sub>, K) - P<sub>T</sub><br/>Minimum net receipt = Q × K - P<sub>T</sub></div>

Where Q is foreign amount, S<sub>T</sub> and K are home currency per foreign unit, and P<sub>T</sub> is **total home-currency premium at the comparison date**.

- **First:** choose the higher selling price, spot or strike.
- **Then:** multiply by the amount for gross receipts.
- **Finally:** deduct premium. The net floor is lower than the gross strike receipt: insurance is not free.

### 4.3 Importer: a call sets a cost ceiling

- The importer must buy Q units. If S<sub>T</sub> &gt; K, exercise and buy at K; if S<sub>T</sub> &lt; K, buy at cheaper spot instead.

<div class="formula">Call-hedged total cost = Q × min(S<sub>T</sub>, K) + P<sub>T</sub><br/>Maximum total cost = Q × K + P<sub>T</sub></div>

The variables use the same units as above.

- **First:** choose the lower buying price.
- **Then:** multiply by Q for the currency purchase cost.
- **Finally:** add premium. A bought option adds to importer cost, never reduces it.

### 4.4 Compare money at the same date

- If premium is paid today but cash flows are compared at maturity, follow the exercise's financing/opportunity-cost convention.
- With simple annual rate r and horizon t years: **P<sub>T</sub> = P<sub>0</sub> × (1 + r × t)**. P<sub>0</sub> is today's total premium. Compute the period rate r×t, then multiply today's cost by one plus that rate.
- Aidan and Boeing importer use this adjustment. Boeing exporter and recap deduct their stated premiums directly; no extra financing rate is supplied there.
- **Strike decides exercise. Strategy break-even compares net outcomes** and therefore depends on premium and forward price too. The examples derive these separately.

## 5. Aidan: protect a GBP receivable in EUR

### 5.1 Translate the sale into an exposure

- European exporter Aidan sells equipment to Regency for **GBP 1,000,000**. Sale in March, collection in June: three months.
- The sale is large relative to Aidan's business; it has no other current foreign customers. A weak pound can materially reduce its euro receipt.
- Aidan receives pounds and wants euros: **sell GBP forward or buy a GBP put**.

| Input | Value | Function |
|---|---|---|
| Receivable Q | GBP 1,000,000 | June amount to convert |
| Spot S<sub>0</sub> | EUR 1.09/GBP | Today's value for pricing the premium |
| Forward F | EUR 1.076/GBP | Fixed June selling price |
| Put strike K | EUR 1.07/GBP | Optional June selling price |
| Premium | 1.5% | Percent of spot-valued notional |
| Annual cost of capital | 12% | Carry option cost to June |
| Forecast spot | EUR 1.085/GBP | Management's view, not the strike |

### 5.2 The forward fixes receipts

- **Step 1:** sell GBP 1m forward for June, matching the receivable's amount and date.
- **Step 2:** 1,000,000 × 1.076 = **EUR 1,076,000**.
- **Step 3:** interpret. Spot can end at 1.00 or 1.20; the pounds still sell at 1.076. Aidan has certainty but no benefit from a stronger pound.

### 5.3 Price the put before calculating its payoff

- **Step 1 - Value the notional:** GBP 1m × EUR 1.09/GBP = EUR 1,090,000 today.
- **Step 2 - Apply 1.5%:** 1,090,000 × 0.015 = **EUR 16,350 paid today**. This is not EUR 0.015 per pound.
- **Step 3 - Period rate:** 12% × 3/12 = **3%**.
- **Step 4 - Carry to June:** 16,350 × 1.03 = **EUR 16,840.50**.
- **Step 5 - Per-pound maturity cost:** 16,840.50/1m = **EUR 0.0168405/GBP**.

<div class="formula">Net June receipt = 1,000,000 × max(S<sub>T</sub>, 1.07) - 16,840.50</div>

Here S<sub>T</sub> is EUR/GBP at collection. Choose the higher selling price, convert the GBP 1m, then deduct the maturity premium.

- **Weak pound, spot 1.00:** exercise. Gross = 1m × 1.07 = EUR 1,070,000; net = 1,070,000 - 16,840.50 = **EUR 1,053,159.50**.
- **Strong pound, spot 1.10:** do not exercise. Sell at spot for EUR 1,100,000; deduct the same premium: **EUR 1,083,159.50**.
- The option protects downside while retaining upside. It does not make the premium disappear in the favourable branch.

<pdf:nextpage />

### 5.4 Floor and break-even are different calculations

**The floor is the worst net receipt under the put.**

- Below 1.07, exercise guarantees EUR 1,070,000 gross. Deduct 16,840.50 for a **EUR 1,053,159.50 net floor**.
- The forward guarantees EUR 1,076,000, higher by **EUR 22,840.50**. The option's advantage is possible upside, not a better guaranteed minimum.

**Break-even asks when that upside makes the put equal to the forward.**

- On the non-exercise branch, net receipt = 1,000,000 × S<sub>BE</sub> - 16,840.50.
- Set **1,000,000 × S<sub>BE</sub> - 16,840.50 = 1,076,000**.
- Add premium to both sides, then divide by GBP 1m: **S<sub>BE</sub> = EUR 1.0928405/GBP**.
- The candidate is above strike 1.07, so this branch is valid. Below strike, the flat option floor never equals the forward receipt.

| Spot (EUR/GBP) | No hedge (EUR) | Forward (EUR) | Put net (EUR) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.00 | 1,000,000 | 1,076,000 | 1,053,159.50 |
| 1.07 | 1,070,000 | 1,076,000 | 1,053,159.50 |
| 1.10 | 1,100,000 | 1,076,000 | 1,083,159.50 |
| 1.20 | 1,200,000 | 1,076,000 | 1,183,159.50 |

- **Exercise below 1.07; forecast 1.085; option beats forward above 1.0928405.** A forecast is a view, not a contract term.
- At forecast 1.085, put net = 1,085,000 - 16,840.50 = **EUR 1,068,159.50**, below the forward. Expecting appreciation does not by itself prove the put gives a better net outcome.

### 5.5 Understand the money-market line in the source chart

This alternative replaces future uncertain conversion with conversion **today**.

- **Step 1 - Borrow pounds now:** GBP borrowing rate is 10% annually, or 2.5% for three months. Borrow 1m/1.025 = **GBP 975,609.76** against June collection.
- **Step 2 - Convert now:** 975,609.76 × 1.09 = **EUR 1,063,414.63**. Future FX movements no longer change these euros.
- **Step 3 - Repay in June:** the customer's GBP 1m pays the GBP loan plus interest exactly. Do not count it again as euro revenue.
- **Step 4 - Carry euros under the chosen assumption:** the chart uses Aidan's 12% annual opportunity return, giving a three-month factor 1.03. Result = 1,063,414.63 × 1.03 = **EUR 1,095,317.07**.
- At the stated 6% annual **deposit rate**, the factor would be 1.015 and proceeds **EUR 1,079,365.85**. These are different carrying assumptions. The opportunity-value line is not the cash earned by a 6% deposit.

Aidan's final choice combines risk tolerance, its FX view and confidence in that view. Boeing applies the same logic first to a receivable, then to a separate payable.

## 6. Boeing exporter: a forward versus a put

### 6.1 Identify the contract

- Boeing sells a landing gear system to British Airways for **GBP 10m**, collectible in one year. Home currency is USD; a weak pound reduces dollars received.
- Forward F = **USD 1.46/GBP**. Put K = **USD 1.46/GBP**. Premium = **USD 0.02/GBP**.
- The slides deduct the premium directly in this comparison. No extra financing adjustment is supplied.

### 6.2 Build each outcome

**Forward: fixed selling price**

- Sell GBP 10m forward. Receipt = 10,000,000 × 1.46 = **USD 14,600,000**.

**Put: floor plus upside, less protection cost**

- Total premium = 10,000,000 × 0.02 = **USD 200,000**.
- Below 1.46, exercise: gross receipt USD 14.6m; subtract USD 0.2m: **USD 14.4m net floor**.
- Above 1.46, sell at spot and deduct USD 0.2m. An extra USD 0.01/GBP raises receipts by 10m × 0.01 = USD 100,000.

<div class="formula">Forward receipt = USD 14,600,000<br/>Put net receipt = 10,000,000 × max(S<sub>T</sub>, 1.46) - 200,000</div>

Here S<sub>T</sub> is USD/GBP at collection. Select the selling price; multiply by GBP 10m; deduct the whole premium.

### 6.3 Derive the break-even

- The put floor is below the forward receipt. Equality must occur on the rising branch above strike.
- Set **10,000,000 × S<sub>BE</sub> - 200,000 = 14,600,000**.
- Add premium and divide: S<sub>BE</sub> = 14,800,000/10,000,000 = **USD 1.48/GBP**.
- At **1.46**, spot equals strike but the put still yields USD 0.2m less than the forward.
- At **1.48**, the extra USD 0.02/GBP obtained at spot pays for that premium relative to the forward.

| Spot (USD/GBP) | No hedge (USD m) | Forward (USD m) | Put net (USD m) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.40 | 14.00 | 14.60 | 14.40 |
| 1.46 | 14.60 | 14.60 | 14.40 |
| 1.48 | 14.80 | 14.60 | 14.60 |
| 1.55 | 15.50 | 14.60 | 15.30 |

- The source graph shows **total dollar proceeds**, not standalone option payoff: forward flat at USD 14.6m; put flat at USD 14.4m until 1.46, then rising and crossing at 1.48.
- For the higher guaranteed receipt, favour the forward. To retain gains from a sufficiently stronger pound, the put can fit, with a lower guaranteed net floor.
- The next case is a different commercial transaction: do not carry this GBP 10m receivable into the importer calculation.

## 7. Boeing importer: a forward versus a call

### 7.1 Reverse the exposure before changing instruments

- Boeing imports a Rolls-Royce engine and owes **GBP 5m in one year**. A stronger pound raises its USD purchase cost.
- Boeing must **buy pounds**: long GBP forward or bought GBP call, not a put.
- Spot USD 1.80/GBP; F = **1.75**; K = **1.80**; premium **USD 0.018/GBP**; US annual rate **6%**. UK 6.5% is also in the source table but is not needed for these forward/option calculations.

### 7.2 Separate purchase cost and insurance cost

- **Forward:** buy GBP 5m at 1.75. Cost = 5,000,000 × 1.75 = **USD 8,750,000**.
- **Premium today:** 5,000,000 × 0.018 = **USD 90,000**.
- **Premium at one year:** 90,000 × 1.06 = **USD 95,400**, or **USD 0.01908/GBP**.
- Compare maturity cash flows: add 95,400 to the currency cost, not today's 90,000.

<div class="formula">Call total cost = 5,000,000 × min(S<sub>T</sub>, 1.80) + 95,400<br/>Maximum cost = 5,000,000 × 1.80 + 95,400 = USD 9,095,400</div>

S<sub>T</sub> is USD/GBP in one year. Choose the cheaper buying price; convert GBP 5m; add the maturity premium.

**Two fully worked scenarios**

- **Spot 1.65:** spot is cheaper than strike, so do not exercise. Buy GBP 5m for 5m × 1.65 = USD 8.25m. Add 95,400: **USD 8,345,400**, cheaper than forward by **USD 404,600**.
- **Spot 1.90:** exercise to buy at 1.80 rather than 1.90. Purchase USD 9m + premium 95,400 = **USD 9,095,400**. The call limits the bill, but the forward costs less in this scenario.

### 7.3 Derive break-even on the valid branch

- Call ceiling exceeds forward cost. Equality can only occur on the lower-spot, non-exercise branch.
- Set **5,000,000 × S<sub>BE</sub> + 95,400 = 8,750,000**.
- Subtract premium and divide: S<sub>BE</sub> = 8,654,600/5m = **USD 1.73092/GBP**.
- This is below 1.80, confirming the branch is valid. Call costs less below 1.73092; forward costs less above it.

| Spot (USD/GBP) | Forward cost (USD) | Call total cost (USD) |
|---|---|---|
| 1.65 | 8,750,000 | 8,345,400 |
| 1.73092 | 8,750,000 | 8,750,000 |
| 1.80 or higher | 8,750,000 | 9,095,400 |

- The call total-cost line rises until strike 1.80, then is flat at the ceiling. The forward line is flat at USD 8.75m throughout.
- **Exercise boundary 1.80; strategy break-even 1.73092.** These answer different questions.
- So far, hedge and commercial amounts matched. The next case shows why that condition cannot be assumed for forecast sales.

## 8. Over-hedging: the sale shrinks, the contract does not

### 8.1 A budget is not a certain receipt

- **T0 - Budget:** EUR-based exporter expects USD 100 revenue and EUR 70 costs. Budget quote is **USD 1.30/EUR**, indirect.
- Exact expected revenue = 100/1.30 = EUR 76.9231. Margin = 76.9231 - 70 = **EUR 6.9231**.
- The slide rounds the reciprocal to EUR 0.77/USD, hence EUR 77 revenue and EUR 7 margin. Keep full decimals until the final answer.
- If sales stay USD 100, spot 1.60 gives margin 100/1.60 - 70 = **EUR -7.50**; spot 1.00 gives **EUR 30**. These are price effects assuming the same quantity sold.

<div class="def"><b>Over-hedging:</b> derivative notional exceeds actual exposure. The excess derivative has no matching commercial cash flow and becomes a speculative position.</div>

### 8.2 Follow every cash flow when sales fall

- At T0, sell **USD 100 forward** at USD 1.30/EUR.
- **T1 - Invoicing:** actual sales are only **USD 50**. The contract still requires USD 100: USD 50 is covered by the sale, USD 50 is uncovered.
- **T2 - Collection:** spot is **USD 1.00/EUR**. The dollar strengthened, but the contract amount has not shrunk.

| Collection step | Calculation | EUR cash flow |
|---|---|---|
| Sell customer dollars under forward | USD 50/1.30 | +38.4615 |
| Buy missing dollars to meet remaining obligation | USD 50/1.00 | -50.0000 |
| Deliver those bought dollars under forward | USD 50/1.30 | +38.4615 |
| Total currency receipt | 38.4615 - 50 + 38.4615 | **26.9231** |
| Subtract operating costs | 26.9231 - 70 | **Margin -43.0769** |

- Uncovered USD 50 costs EUR 50 to acquire but brings EUR 38.4615 through the forward. **Excess-hedge loss = EUR 11.5385**.
- Genuine sale receipt minus excess loss minus costs = 38.4615 - 11.5385 - 70 = **EUR -43.0769**, approximately the slide's -43.
- The hedge still matched the actual sale on USD 50. The problem is the other USD 50: no sale offsets that derivative obligation.

### 8.3 Explain what the total loss contains

- Budget margin 6.9231 to final margin -43.0769 is **EUR 50 deterioration**.
- **Revenue shortfall at locked rate:** USD 50/1.30 = EUR 38.4615. **Excess-hedge loss:** EUR 11.5385. Together: EUR 50.
- Do not call all EUR 50 a derivative loss: quantity risk and excess-hedge loss are distinct.
- Design questions: **when** to hedge (budget or invoicing), and **how much** (100% or less). Fixing an FX price does not fix sales volume.

## 9. Recap: budget, invoicing and collection

### 9.1 Put the time line and units in order

- EUR-based exporter: expected **USD 100 at T0**, invoice **USD 100 at T1**, collection at **T2**. Unlike over-hedging, quantity does not fall.
- At T0: do nothing; sell forward at **USD 1.54/EUR**; or buy a put on USD.
- If unhedged at T0, at T1: remain unhedged or sell forward at **USD 1.65/EUR**.
- T2 scenarios: **USD 1.40/EUR** (strong dollar, favourable), or **USD 1.75/EUR** (weak dollar, unfavourable).
- Use the worked solution's **USD 1.5758/EUR** initial/strike quote consistently. Earlier setup rounds it to 1.57; do not mix that rounded input into the precise solution.

### 9.2 Build the option calculation before the comparison

- **Step 1 - Direct strike:** K = 1/1.5758 = **EUR 0.6345983/USD**. This is the protected euro selling price.
- **Step 2 - Total premium:** USD 0.02 per USD sold × USD 100 = **USD 2**.
- **Step 3 - Convert premium at T0:** 2/1.5758 = **EUR 1.2691966**. This exercise deducts it directly, without a financing adjustment.
- **Step 4 - Good scenario 1.40:** direct spot 1/1.40 = 0.7142857 EUR/USD exceeds strike. Do not exercise. Net = 100/1.40 - 1.2691966 = **EUR 70.1594**.
- **Step 5 - Bad scenario 1.75:** direct spot 1/1.75 = 0.5714286 is below strike. Exercise. Net = 100/1.5758 - 1.2691966 = **EUR 62.1906**.

<div class="formula">Option net receipt = 100 × max(1/R<sub>T</sub>, 1/1.5758) - 2/1.5758</div>

R<sub>T</sub> is indirect USD/EUR spot. Take reciprocals before comparing selling prices; choose the higher EUR/USD price; convert USD 100; deduct the euro premium.

### 9.3 Compare all strategies on the same basis

| Realised receipts in EUR | Strong dollar: 1.40 | Weak dollar: 1.75 |
|---|---|---|
| No hedge | 100/1.40 = **71.4286** | 100/1.75 = **57.1429** |
| T0 forward | 100/1.54 = **64.9351** | **64.9351** |
| T1 forward | 100/1.65 = **60.6061** | **60.6061** |
| Put, net of premium | **70.1594** | **62.1906** |

- **No hedge:** best in the favourable scenario, worst in the unfavourable one. The wide spread is the risk hedging reduces.
- **T0 forward:** fixed receipt higher than the put floor, but no stronger-dollar upside.
- **Put:** keeps most favourable FX gain after paying for protection. Only its floor is guaranteed, not the favourable outcome.
- **T1 forward:** fixes less revenue because the available quote is worse for selling dollars. It still protects against later adverse moves; it cannot reverse a movement already experienced.

- Consistent T0 quote 1.5758 gives gross budget receipts **100/1.5758 = EUR 63.4598**. The source's standalone EUR 62.5 label is not that conversion. The table uses one coherent set of inputs.
- These are **receipts, not operating margins**: recap supplies no operating cost to subtract. Do not import EUR 70 costs from the separate over-hedging exercise.

### 9.4 Receipts, effective rates and budget are not interchangeable

- **Market spot** is a price before option cost. **Net effective rate** is final receipts per foreign unit after premium.
- Good scenario: EUR 70.1594/USD 100 = **EUR 0.701594/USD**. Reciprocal **USD 1.425326/EUR**, not market spot 1.40.
- Floor: EUR 62.1906/USD 100 = **EUR 0.621906/USD**; reciprocal **USD 1.607959/EUR**.
- Divide net home receipts by foreign notional first; invert only to obtain the indirect effective quote.
## 10. Choose a hedge for the firm's objective

### 10.1 Judge the distribution, not one favourable scenario

<div class="def"><b>Hedging:</b> reduces variability of expected cash flows about the mean of their distribution. In the lecture, reducing that variance reduces risk.</div>

- No hedge leaves cash flows moving with FX. A good scenario may be attractive; a bad one may disrupt financing or operating plans.
- A matched forward fixes the conversion price. Certainty comes with forgone favourable FX movements.
- A bought option protects downside while retaining upside. Premium lowers exporter net receipts or raises importer total cost.
- Compare the guaranteed result, possible upside, protection cost and ability to absorb a bad outcome, not just the biggest number in one column.

### 10.2 Management judgement matters

- Aidan's decision combines **risk tolerance, FX view and confidence in that view**. A forecast does not make risk disappear.
- A firm prioritising a known cash amount can favour forward. A firm paying for flexibility can favour option, accepting its lower net floor or higher cost ceiling.
- The recap's reasons to stay unhedged include competitors not hedging; FX treated as core business risk; enough liquidity or debt/equity access to absorb losses; or shareholder diversification where exposure creates no indirect costs for the firm.
- Those are reasons to assess the situation, not proof that no hedge is always best. The firm's cash flow still bears the risk.

### 10.3 Place FX risk within financial risk management

- The lecture names market, credit, liquidity, operational, foreign exchange, interest rate, business, reputational and commodity risks. They are connected.
- A2A links **liquidity risk** to financial planning/pre-funding; **derating risk** to asset rotation and equity/hybrid financing; **interest rate risk** to the fixed/variable debt balance; and **sustainability covenant risk** to a sustainable financial committee.


### 10.4 Keep price and quantity risk together

- Budget hedging protects an earlier price but forecast amount may change. Invoice hedging uses a clearer contract amount but the rate may already have worsened.
- Hedging 100% of a forecast does not eliminate every risk. An excess derivative can become an uncovered obligation.
- A defensible recommendation identifies the cash flow, protection sought, matched amount/date and remaining risk. "Derivatives reduce risk" alone is not an explanation.

## 11. Common mistakes & exam traps

- **Wrong conversion:** USD ÷ (USD/EUR) or USD × (EUR/USD) gives EUR. Multiplying by USD/EUR does not.
- **Wrong appreciation label:** rising USD/EUR is strong EUR, weak USD. Rising EUR/USD means strong USD.
- **Wrong instrument:** receivable = short forward/bought put; payable = long forward/bought call on foreign currency.
- **Standalone derivative mistaken for total outcome:** a forward loss may offset a favourable business movement. Do not double-count payoff alongside physical settlement.
- **Gross strike mistaken for net protection:** put floor = QK minus premium; call ceiling = QK plus premium.
- **Premium disappears when unused:** false. It is paid in both exercise and non-exercise branches.
- **Strike confused with strategy break-even:** Boeing exporter boundaries are 1.46 and 1.48; Boeing importer 1.80 and 1.73092. Exercise and net-outcome comparison are different questions.
- **Percentage confused with per-unit premium:** Aidan's 1.5% applies to spot-valued notional. Boeing's 0.02 and 0.018 are USD per GBP.
- **Money dates mixed:** use Aidan maturity premium 16,840.50 and Boeing importer 95,400. Follow other examples' supplied direct-premium convention.
- **Cases mixed:** Aidan uses EUR; Boeing uses USD. Boeing exporter receives GBP 10m; importer pays GBP 5m. Recap sells USD 100.
- **Quantity risk forgotten:** the forward fixes price on its notional, not the sales amount. Lower sales do not cancel delivery obligations.
- **EUR 50 all called derivative loss:** over-hedge loss is EUR 11.5385; EUR 50 is total budget-margin deterioration, including revenue shortfall.
- **Rounded inputs mixed:** keep one quote consistently and round only at the end, especially reciprocals and recap 1.5758.

## 12. Must know absolutely

<div class="key"><b>The complete logic</b><br/>&bull; Identify home currency, foreign amount, receipt/payment and settlement date.<br/>&bull; Read quote units. Direct and indirect quotations are reciprocals.<br/>&bull; Receive foreign currency: short forward or bought put. Pay foreign currency: long forward or bought call.<br/>&bull; A matched forward fixes the home-currency amount.<br/>&bull; Put: higher selling price minus premium. Call: lower buying price plus premium.<br/>&bull; Strike decides exercise. Equality of net outcomes decides forward-option break-even.<br/>&bull; Put premiums into the right currency and apply the stated time convention.<br/>&bull; Judge underlying and derivative together. Excess hedging creates an uncovered speculative position.</div>
