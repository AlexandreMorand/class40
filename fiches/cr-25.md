<div class="key"><b>How to read Dr and Cr (journal entries)</b><br/>
&bull; <b>Dr = Debit</b> means the <b>left</b> column. <b>Cr = Credit</b> means the <b>right</b> column. It is only a side of the page. It does not mean "bad" or "good", and it does not mean cash in or out.<br/>
&bull; Every entry touches at least two accounts and the <b>total Dr always equals the total Cr</b>. If they differ, the entry is wrong.<br/>
&bull; <b>Dr increases</b> an asset (cash, inventory, receivable, equipment) or an expense (cost, loss). <b>Cr increases</b> a liability (payable, debt), equity (share capital, reserves) or revenue.<br/>
&bull; The opposite side reduces them. A Cr on an asset means the asset goes down (cash paid out, inventory used). A Dr on a liability means the debt goes down (you repay it).<br/>
&bull; Quick check, ask two questions: what did the company <b>get</b> (Dr) and what did it <b>give or owe</b> (Cr)? Example: buying goods on credit for 100 is Dr Inventory 100 (it got goods) and Cr Trade payables 100 (it now owes the supplier).</div>

## 1. IFRS 2 Scope and the Two Types of Share-Based Payment

- Share-based payments are regulated by IFRS 2, which applies only to share-based payments made for the acquisition of goods or services — it does <b>not</b> apply to dividends or to a general issue of shares (e.g. a rights issue for cash).

<div class="def"><b>Share-based payment transaction:</b> a transaction in which an entity receives goods or services in exchange for either (a) equity instruments, or (b) incurring a liability.</div>

<div class="def"><b>Equity-settled share-based payment:</b> the entity issues its own shares (or share options) as payment for goods or services. The goods/services received are measured at fair value and recorded as an asset or expense, with a corresponding increase in equity.</div>

<div class="def"><b>Cash-settled share-based payment:</b> the entity agrees to transfer cash or other assets, in amounts based on the price of its own shares, in return for goods/services received.</div>

## 2. Measurement: Goods/Services vs. Equity Instruments

<div class="def"><b>Measurement hierarchy:</b> transactions where goods/services are received for equity instruments are, in principle, measured at the fair value of the goods or services received. Only if that fair value cannot be measured reliably is the fair value of the equity instruments granted used instead.</div>

- Timing of the fair value estimate also depends on which measurement basis applies: for transactions measured at the fair value of the goods/services received, fair value is estimated at the <b>date of receipt</b>; for transactions measured at the fair value of the equity instruments granted, fair value is estimated at <b>grant date</b>.

<div class="example"><b>Equity-settled example:</b> on 1 December 2010, Company A issues 10,000 shares to Company B for goods with a fair value of €15,000; the goods sit in inventory at year-end and are sold for €20,000 during 2011. The three entries are below.</div>
<p><b>1 December 2010, receipt of the goods</b></p>

<div class="def"><b>In plain words:</b> the company now owns goods worth 15,000 (an asset goes up, so Dr). It paid with new shares, so equity goes up (Cr). No cash moved.</div>

<p><b>During 2011, the goods are sold</b></p>

<table><tr><th width="52%">Account</th><th width="24%">Debit (Dr)</th><th width="24%">Credit (Cr)</th></tr><tr><td>COGS (cost of goods sold)</td><td align="right">15,000</td><td></td></tr><tr><td>&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Inventory</td><td></td><td align="right">15,000</td></tr><tr><td>Cash / Receivables</td><td align="right">20,000</td><td></td></tr><tr><td>&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Revenue</td><td></td><td align="right">20,000</td></tr></table>

<div class="def"><b>In plain words:</b> the goods leave the warehouse, so inventory goes down (Cr) and their cost becomes an expense (Dr COGS). The customer pays 20,000, so cash goes up (Dr) and revenue is earned (Cr).</div>


<div class="example"><b>Cash-settled example:</b> same facts, but Company A instead pays cash based on the fair value of 10,000 of its own equity shares, which was €15,000 at 1 December 2010 (receipt date) but had risen to €17,000 by 31 December 2010 (year-end). The entries are below.</div>
<p><b>1 December 2010, receipt of the goods</b></p>

<table><tr><th width="52%">Account</th><th width="24%">Debit (Dr)</th><th width="24%">Credit (Cr)</th></tr><tr><td>Inventory</td><td align="right">15,000</td><td></td></tr><tr><td>&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Trade payables</td><td></td><td align="right">15,000</td></tr></table>

<div class="def"><b>In plain words:</b> the company got goods worth 15,000 (asset up, Dr). It did not pay yet and owes the supplier an amount of cash equal to the value of 10,000 of its shares, so a liability goes up (Cr).</div>

<p><b>31 December 2010, year-end remeasurement</b></p>

<table><tr><th width="52%">Account</th><th width="24%">Debit (Dr)</th><th width="24%">Credit (Cr)</th></tr><tr><td>Loss</td><td align="right">2,000</td><td></td></tr><tr><td>&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Trade payables</td><td></td><td align="right">2,000</td></tr></table>

<div class="def"><b>In plain words:</b> the debt is linked to the share price, which rose from 15,000 to 17,000. The company now owes 2,000 more, so the liability goes up (Cr) and the extra cost is a loss in profit or loss (Dr). This is the key difference with the equity-settled case, where nothing is remeasured.</div>

<p><b>During 2011, payment and sale</b></p>

<table><tr><th width="52%">Account</th><th width="24%">Debit (Dr)</th><th width="24%">Credit (Cr)</th></tr><tr><td>Trade payables</td><td align="right">17,000</td><td></td></tr><tr><td>&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Cash</td><td></td><td align="right">17,000</td></tr><tr><td>COGS (cost of goods sold)</td><td align="right">15,000</td><td></td></tr><tr><td>&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Inventory</td><td></td><td align="right">15,000</td></tr><tr><td>Cash / Receivables</td><td align="right">20,000</td><td></td></tr><tr><td>&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Revenue</td><td></td><td align="right">20,000</td></tr></table>

<div class="def"><b>In plain words:</b> first the debt of 17,000 is paid, so the liability disappears (Dr) and cash goes down (Cr). Then the goods are sold exactly as in the equity-settled example, cost as an expense and 20,000 of revenue.</div>


<div class="watch"><b>Watch out:</b> the key structural difference between the two examples is that a cash-settled liability is <b>remeasured to fair value at each reporting date</b> until settlement (generating a further P&amp;L gain/loss each period), whereas an equity-settled transaction's initial measurement, once made, does <b>not</b> get remeasured for subsequent share price movements — this distinction becomes central again in Section 5.</div>

## 3. The Anatomy of an Executive Stock Option Scheme

- Executive stock option schemes are the most common form of equity-settled share-based payment, giving executives the right to buy shares at a predetermined (exercise) price over a specified period — often used by growth companies (e.g. technology) to motivate employees, since the option becomes valuable if the share price climbs above the exercise price during the option's life.

<div class="def"><b>Three key dates:</b> the <b>grant date</b> (when the option is awarded); the <b>vesting (service) date</b> (the end of the service period over which employee services are consumed, e.g. 3 years); and the <b>maturity date</b> (the end of the period during which the vested option can be exercised, assuming any performance conditions were satisfied).</div>

## 4. Vesting Conditions vs. Non-Vesting Conditions

<div class="def"><b>Vesting condition:</b> a condition that determines whether the entity receives the services entitling the counterparty to the share-based payment.</div>

<div class="key"><b>Must know (the vesting-condition taxonomy):</b><br/>&bull; A vesting condition is either a <b>service condition</b> (completing a specified period of service, explicit or implicit) and/or a <b>performance condition</b> (meeting specified performance targets while rendering service)<br/>&bull; A performance target is a <b>non-market condition</b> if defined by reference to the entity's own (or a group entity's) operations/activities<br/>&bull; A performance target is a <b>market condition</b> if defined by reference to the price/value of the entity's (or a group entity's) equity instruments, including shares and share options</div>

<div class="def"><b>Decision test (is a condition a vesting condition at all?):</b> does the condition determine whether the entity receives the services entitling the counterparty to the payment? If no, it is a <b>non-vesting condition</b>. If yes, and it requires only a specified period of service, it is a <b>service condition</b>; if it requires more (a performance target), classify it as a <b>non-market</b> or <b>market</b> performance condition per the taxonomy above.</div>

## 5. Recognition and Measurement of Stock Option Cost

<div class="key"><b>Must know (IFRS 2's recognition timing rule):</b><br/>&bull; <b>No vesting conditions:</b> the employee expense is recognised <b>immediately</b>, at grant date<br/>&bull; <b>Vesting conditions exist:</b> the expense is recognised <b>over the vesting period</b> (e.g. the minimum required service period)</div>

<div class="key"><b>Must know (IFRS 2's measurement rule):</b><br/>&bull; The cost is measured as the <b>fair value of the share options at grant date</b> &mdash; fixed once, at the start<br/>&bull; <b>Market conditions</b> occurring after the grant date (e.g. subsequent share price movements) are <b>not</b> taken into account when determining the P&amp;L charge over the vesting period &mdash; the grant-date fair value estimate already builds in the probability distribution of future share prices, so it is not revisited</div>

## 6. Worked Examples: No Vesting Conditions vs. a Service Condition

<div class="example"><b>No vesting conditions (Example 1, ordinary shares):</b> on 31 December 20X1, Entity A grants each employee 10 ordinary shares, with no vesting conditions. Because employees have an unconditional right to the shares immediately at grant date, Entity A recognises the full staff cost (and the corresponding increase in equity) on 31 December 20X1 itself.</div>

<div class="example"><b>No vesting conditions (Example 2, options):</b> Entity A grants 10 share options per employee to reward past performance, exercisable any time after 31 December 20X2, with no further conditions to be met (even an employee who leaves before that date keeps the right to exercise later). Because there is no vesting condition (departing employees are not excluded), the full cost is again recognised at grant date, 31 December 20X1.</div>

<div class="example"><b>With a service condition (Example 3):</b> Entity A grants 10 share options per employee, but exercise is conditional on the employee working throughout 20X2 — a service condition. Because the services being paid for are rendered over 20X2 (the vesting period), Entity A recognises the staff cost and the corresponding equity increase during 20X2 (not at the 31 December 20X1 grant date).</div>

<div class="example"><b>Measuring at grant-date fair value (Example 4):</b> Entity A grants employees ordinary shares worth €5,000 in total fair value at grant date (31 December 20X1), with no vesting conditions. The full expense is recognised immediately (entry below). The measurement basis is explicitly the fair value of the shares (not of the employee services), at grant date.</div>

<p><b>31 December 20X1, grant date</b></p>

<table><tr><th width="52%">Account</th><th width="24%">Debit (Dr)</th><th width="24%">Credit (Cr)</th></tr><tr><td>Profit or loss (staff expense)</td><td align="right">5,000</td><td></td></tr><tr><td>&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Equity</td><td></td><td align="right">5,000</td></tr></table>

<div class="def"><b>In plain words:</b> the employees received shares instead of cash, so the company records the cost as an expense (Dr). Nothing is owed in cash, so the other side is equity going up (Cr).</div>

## 7. Full Worked Example: A Multi-Year Vesting Schedule

<div class="example"><b>Setup:</b> on 1 January 2011, M plc grants each of 4 executives options to purchase 25,000 shares at 25 cents/share (the exercise price). Share value at grant date = €1; nominal value of shares = 10 cents; fair value of each option at grant date = 75 cents. The vesting condition requires 3 years of service from 1 January 2011.</div>

<div class="example"><b>Annual expense (Solution 5):</b> total grant-date fair value of all options = 4 executives &times; 25,000 options &times; €0.75 = €75,000, spread evenly over the 3-year vesting period: €75,000 &divide; 3 = <b>€25,000 per year</b>. The journal entry for each of 31 December 2011, 2012 and 2013 is below. Note that the subsequent share price rises (€1.50 in 2012, €2 in 2013, €3 in 2014) are <b>irrelevant</b> to this calculation, per Section 5's rule that market conditions after grant date do not affect the P&amp;L charge.</div>

<div class="def"><b>In plain words:</b> one third of the total cost is booked as an expense each year (Dr). The matching credit builds up a stock options reserve inside equity. No cash and no liability are involved.</div>

The entry at exercise is below. Share capital is 4 &times; 25,000 &times; €0.10 nominal value, and share premium is the balancing figure.</div>

Dr total 100,000 equals Cr total 100,000.</div>

## 8. Forfeiture and Expiry

<div class="key"><b>Must know (what happens when a vesting condition is not met):</b><br/>&bull; <b>Failure to meet a service condition or a non-market performance condition:</b> the expense is <b>revised</b> to reflect the best available estimate of the number of equity instruments actually expected to vest (i.e. forfeited awards are backed out of the cumulative expense)<br/>&bull; <b>Failure to meet a market condition:</b> <b>no change</b> to the accounting &mdash; the entity continues recognising the expense over the remainder of the vesting period exactly as originally planned, since market-condition probabilities were already built into the grant-date fair value</div>

<div class="example"><b>Example 6, one executive leaves during the vesting period (Solution 6):</b> using the same facts as Example 5, but one of the four executives leaves on 1 January 2012, losing their options (a service-condition forfeiture, so the estimate is revised). 31 December 2011 charge remains €25,000 (before the departure is known, since it occurs on 1 January 2012). 31 December 2012 charge = 12,500 — computed either as [(3 executives &times; 25,000 &times; 0.75 &times; 2/3) &minus; 25,000 already recognised] = 12,500, or equivalently per-executive: 25,000&divide;4 = 6,250/year-executive, so (3 &times; 6,250) &minus; 6,250 = 12,500. 31 December 2013 charge = 18,750 (= 3 &times; 6,250, the remaining 3 executives' full annual share). At exercise by the 3 remaining executives, the entry is below. Cash is 3 &times; 25,000 &times; 0.25, the reserve is 25,000 + 12,500 + 18,750, and share capital is 3 &times; 25,000 &times; 0.10.</div>

<p><b>Exercise by the 3 remaining executives</b></p>

<div class="def"><b>In plain words:</b> same logic as before. Cash received plus the reserve cleared (75,000 in total) equals the new shares at nominal value (7,500) plus the share premium (67,500).</div>

There is <b>no change</b> to the company's assets, liabilities or total equity — the expense already recognised over the vesting period is not reversed merely because the vested option was never exercised.</div>

<div class="example"><b>Example 7, performance condition with a variable vesting period (updated handout):</b> at the beginning of year 1, Benning Ltd grants 100 shares to each of its 50 employees, conditional on staying through a three-year vesting period. Fair value at grant date is &euro;20 per share and no dividends are expected. The shares vest early if earnings grow by more than 18% (end of year 1), by more than 13% a year on average (end of year 2) or by more than 10% a year on average (end of year 3). Year 1: earnings grew 14% and three employees left; the company expects the shares to vest at the end of year 2 and a further three departures, so 44 employees will vest. Year 2: earnings grew 10%, an average of 12% (14% and 10%), so the shares do <b>not</b> vest; two employees left; the company now expects another two departures in year 3 and an average of 10% over three years. Year 3: earnings grew 8%, an average of 10.67%, so the condition is met; three more employees left; the 42 remaining (50 &minus; [3 + 2 + 3]) each receive 100 shares. The handout asks for the annual and cumulative expense in each year but does not print the answer.</div>

- Worked here by applying the method of this session (the answer below is self-constructed, not copied from an official key):
  - the performance condition is a **non-market** one, so the expense is **revised** each year to the best estimate of how many employees will vest and over what expected vesting period;
  - **Year 1:** cumulative expense = 44 &times; 100 &times; &euro;20 &times; 1/2 = &euro;44,000, so the year 1 charge is **&euro;44,000**;
  - **Year 2:** the expected vesting date moves to the end of year 3 and the expected number is 50 &minus; (3 + 2 + 2) = 43. Cumulative expense = 43 &times; 100 &times; &euro;20 &times; 2/3 = &euro;57,333, so the year 2 charge is &euro;57,333 &minus; &euro;44,000 = **&euro;13,333**;
  - **Year 3:** cumulative expense = 42 &times; 100 &times; &euro;20 = &euro;84,000, so the year 3 charge is &euro;84,000 &minus; &euro;57,333 = **&euro;26,667**;
  - the share price after grant date plays no role, because the fair value of &euro;20 is fixed at grant date.

## 9. Disclosure Requirements

<div class="key"><b>Must know (main IFRS 2 disclosures):</b><br/>&bull; The nature and extent of share-based payment arrangements that existed during the period<br/>&bull; How the fair value of the goods/services received, or of the equity instruments granted, was determined<br/>&bull; The effect of share-based payment transactions on the period's profit or loss and on financial position</div>

## 10. Common Mistakes and Exam Traps

- Remeasuring an equity-settled option's cost for subsequent share price movements — grant-date fair value is fixed once and never revisited for market-condition changes; only cash-settled liabilities are remeasured to current fair value at each reporting date.
- Recognising the full option cost at grant date whenever a service condition exists — a genuine vesting condition (service or non-market performance) spreads the cost over the vesting period; only the absence of any vesting condition triggers immediate full recognition.
- Reversing previously-recognised expense when a vested option later expires unexercised — expiry after vesting is a pure equity reclassification with no P&amp;L or asset/liability effect; only forfeiture <i>during</i> the vesting period (failing a service/non-market condition) revises the cumulative expense.
- Revising the cumulative expense for a failed market condition (e.g. a share-price target not met) — market-condition failures get no accounting adjustment at all, unlike service or non-market performance condition failures.
- Confusing which figure is the balancing figure in the exercise-date journal entry — cash received and share capital (at nominal value) are both directly computable; share premium is always the plug that makes the entry balance.

## 11. Must Know Absolutely

<div class="key"><b>Must know:</b><br/>&bull; IFRS 2 covers share-based payment for goods/services only, not dividends or general share issues<br/>&bull; Equity-settled: measure goods/services fair value (or equity instrument fair value if unreliable); cash-settled: recognise a liability, remeasured to fair value each period until settlement<br/>&bull; No vesting condition &rarr; full expense at grant date; vesting condition (service and/or performance) &rarr; expense spread over the vesting period<br/>&bull; Cost is always measured at the fair value of the equity instrument <b>at grant date</b>, fixed regardless of later market-condition/share-price movements<br/>&bull; Forfeiture (service/non-market condition failure) &rarr; revise cumulative expense to the revised number expected to vest; market-condition failure &rarr; no change; expiry after vesting &rarr; pure equity reclassification, zero P&amp;L/net-asset effect</div>
